What Animal Rescue Taught Me About Building Products

How nearly ten years in animal rescue shaped the way I think about real problems, simple systems, unexpected situations and user trust.

Personal essay 03

Themes

Product Thinking Leadership Animal Rescue

For nearly ten years, I helped establish and run an animal rescue centre in China. During that time, our team rescued and cared for more than 1,000 dogs and 100 cats.

Animal rescue may seem very different from software development, but the experience changed how I think about building products.

Start with the Real Problem

In animal rescue, the most urgent problem is not always the most obvious one. A rescued animal may need food and shelter, but it may also need medical treatment, behavioural support or help finding a suitable home.

Product development is similar. Before building a feature, I need to understand what users actually need and what problem the feature is meant to solve.

This has influenced how I approach PetHome, my full-stack pet-care platform. Instead of adding features simply because they sound useful, I think about the real situations faced by pet owners and carers, such as managing bookings, recording care instructions and keeping payment information organised.

Simple Systems Matter

Running a rescue centre involves a large amount of information. We needed to keep track of each animal’s health, medication, feeding requirements, vaccination records and adoption status.

When many animals and volunteers are involved, even a small communication mistake can create a serious problem. A system therefore needs to be clear, reliable and easy for people to use.

I apply the same thinking when designing software. A product does not need to look complicated to be useful. Clear navigation, understandable information and a simple workflow are often more valuable than having many unnecessary features.

Be Ready for Unexpected Situations

Animal rescue rarely goes exactly as planned. An animal’s condition can suddenly change, an adoption may be cancelled or an emergency may require immediate attention.

This taught me to think about situations beyond the normal process. When building software, I now consider questions such as: What happens if a payment fails? What if a booking is cancelled? What if information is missing or incorrect?

Thinking about these cases early helps me build products that are more practical and reliable.

Trust Must Be Earned

People trusted us to care for rescued animals and make responsible adoption decisions. That trust came with a great deal of responsibility.

Software products also depend on trust, especially when they handle personal information, bookings and payments. Users need to know that their information is protected and that the system will work as expected.

This is why I see security, accuracy and clear communication as essential parts of product development, rather than optional improvements.

Building with Purpose

Animal rescue taught me patience, responsibility and how to keep working when resources were limited. It also taught me to listen carefully and focus on solutions that create a real impact.

These lessons continue to shape the way I build software today. Technology and animal rescue are different fields, but both begin with the same question: What problem are we trying to solve, and how can we make life a little easier for the people—or animals—who depend on us?

Lessons that stay with me.

The best products begin with care, responsibility and a clear understanding of who depends on them.
